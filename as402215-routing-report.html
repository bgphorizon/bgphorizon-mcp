AS402215: ninety-four address blocks in eleven days, with the paperwork trailing

Between 3 and 13 August 2026, AS402215 (DEVOLOGY, registered to Devology Tech LLC) announced 94 IPv4 /24 blocks. Only 25 of them stayed in the routing table for the whole window. Ninety-three high-severity detections name AS402215 as the party making a never-before-seen origin claim, displacing 77 other networks, which reads as a serial hijacker until the registration data is checked: in seven of the eight blocks we verified, the registered holder had already changed to one maintainer, netutils-mnt, hours or days before the announcement went out. The better description is a transfer pipeline running ahead of its own cryptographic records. That is not theft, but it produced sixteen dual-origin conflicts and, on 12 August, took a group of these blocks off the internet for 93 minutes.

Terms used in this report
BGP
The internet’s routing system. Networks use it to advertise which blocks of addresses they can deliver traffic for.
AS / AS number
Autonomous System. A numbered network under one administration, written AS402215.
Origin AS
The network that claims to hold the addresses, and the last entry on the route’s path. A prefix normally has exactly one.
Prefix
A block of addresses, written 45.62.168.0/24. A /24 is 256 addresses; a /16 is 65,536.
Upstream / transit
A provider a network buys connectivity from. It is the AS immediately before the origin on the path.
MOAS
Multiple Origin AS. Two networks announcing the same prefix at the same time. Usually harmless, from anycast or multihoming. A new one has the shape of a hijack.
Persistence
Whether a prefix is announced steadily: persistent, intermittent or transient. A block present on 2 days out of 11 is transient, and calling that a migration would be wrong.
RPKI / ROA
Resource PKI. A ROA is a signed record stating that a given AS may originate a given prefix, so routers can reject announcements that do not match. An announcement with no matching ROA is RPKI-invalid.
IRR
Internet Routing Registry. A published database of intended routes that providers use to build filters. Not cryptographically verified; stale entries persist.
Collector / peer / vantage point
RouteViews and RIPE RIS run collectors that receive routes from peer routers. A change seen from only one peer is probably an artifact of measurement rather than a real routing event.
Reachability / outage window
How many observing peers had no route at all, and for how long. This is impact. A raw count of routing messages is not.
01

What the evidence supports

The detection platform recorded 497 anomalous incidents naming AS402215 in this window. Of the 200 returned in the unfiltered page, 169 place AS402215 in the offending role, meaning the direction field reads queried_entity_is_invalid_party. Querying by type to get complete counts gives 153 origin_mismatch_new incidents, of which 93 name AS402215 as the invalid party. All 93 are rated high severity. They span 52 distinct prefixes and displace 77 distinct baseline networks. Every one carries kind: "new", so none is a dormant pairing returning after a lull.

Read on its own, that is a serial hijacker profile. It does not survive contact with the registration data. We ran validate_announcement against eight of the contested blocks. Seven returned the same registered holder, netutils-mnt, flagged recently_transferred: true, with a holder-change timestamp that precedes the announcement. 85.92.140.0/24 changed hands at 03:24 UTC on 10 August and was announced at 07:19 the same morning. 31.77.215.0/24 changed hands at 05:10 on 11 August and was announced at 06:02 the following day. All eight now validate against a ROA naming AS402215.

Assessment

Observation: AS402215 announces blocks whose registered holder changed to netutils-mnt shortly beforehand, and those announcements generate first-time origin claims because the pairing has never existed.

Inference: the most probable reading is an address transfer pipeline moving faster than RPKI and IRR records can follow. A pairing that has never existed is what both a hijack and a same-week transfer look like to a 30-day baseline, and the registration timestamps distinguish them. This is an operational problem with measurable consequences, described in sections 04 and 06, rather than a seizure of other people’s address space.

Correction made during analysis

Our first pass through the detection data concluded that AS402215 was hijacking at scale, on the strength of the 169-to-31 split between offending and victim roles and the scattered, unrelated character of the address space. That conclusion was wrong, and checking the holder records overturned it. Detection direction identifies who made the invalid announcement. It does not establish who holds the addresses, and we had treated it as if it did.

Two findings cut the other way, and neither is resolved by the transfer explanation. The previous origins frequently keep announcing after the paperwork moves, producing genuine simultaneous conflicts. And at least one block was announced into space whose covering ROAs belong to parties bearing no resemblance to the prior holder. Sections 05 and 04 set both out.

02

Persistence: three quarters of this inventory does not stay put

Before anything can be called a migration or a handover, the prefixes have to be classified. The server does this itself, counting days present against days in the window, and its classification is the one used throughout this report. Of the 94 IPv4 /24s AS402215 announced, 25 are persistent, 44 intermittent and 25 transient. There is no IPv6. Every prefix is a /24, and the whole inventory comes to 24,064 addresses.

That distribution is the most important fact here. Twenty-five blocks appeared on one or two days out of eleven and then vanished. When a high-severity detection fires on one of those, the honest reading is that a block flickered into the table for a day.

The persistent quarter behaves differently from the rest. 45.62.168.0/24 is representative: origin_history over the past month shows AS147218 originating it until 18 July, AS402215 taking over that day, and AS402215 holding it continuously since, with AS147293 reappearing alongside on seven separate days. The registration agrees. The holder is a “Private Customer” record last changed on 9 January 2026, marked recently_transferred: false, and validate_announcement returns a clean clear verdict. A settled holding with a noisy neighbour.

Now compare 87.83.115.0/24. AS151177 originated it on 11 August; AS402215 originated it on the 12th and 13th. The server labels that transition a handover with zero days of overlap. But the prefix is classified transient, present on 2 days of 11, with exactly one day of prior history to compare against. Two days is not a migration, and the most we can say is that a block appeared.

03

Attribution: broadly observed, not an artifact

A spike seen through one collector peer is a property of the observatory rather than of the internet, so the volume and the detections both need checking before they can be treated as real.

They hold up. Across the eleven days AS402215 generated 323,984 announcements observed at 24 distinct collectors. Concentration metadata reports a top-collector share of 19.5% for route-views.napafrica and 10.96 effective vantage points. No single_vantage_point or concentration warning was raised on any timeline, path or visibility call made during this investigation. Among the 93 high-severity origin claims, the median incident was seen by 205 peers, and 52 of the 93 were seen by at least 100 peers across at least 10 collectors.

The two volume peaks are similarly broad-based: 65,799 announcements on 10 August and 56,616 on the 12th, against a median of 19,931. A compare_windows run across the collector dimension shows no collector appearing or disappearing between the quiet and busy halves of the window. Every collector simply counted more. The strip below draws the largest single collector as a separate segment so the split is visible: napafrica stays roughly flat while the total triples.

One further calibration. Over the same fortnight platform_baseline reports roughly 181,805 anomalous incidents platform-wide, dominated by irr_invalid_asn and unregistered_route. The internet carries a very large volume of routine registry inconsistency, so 497 incidents from one ASN is a small fraction of the total. What sets these apart is composition rather than count: 93 high-severity first-time origin claims and 18 MOAS conflicts, from a six-month-old ASN, in eleven days.

Sampling limits on the counts above

The unfiltered detections call matched 497 incidents and returned the first 200, and its counts_by_type block describes that page rather than the full set. Every figure quoted here for origin_mismatch_new (153), rpki_invalid_asn (17) and moas_conflict (18) comes from a per-type re-query that returned complete results. Counts for irr_invalid_asn, irr_change and roa_change were not re-queried and are floors rather than totals. The platform_baseline response carries its own sampled_baseline warning: the breakdown is drawn from the 500 most recent incidents, so its proportions are usable but its absolute per-type counts are not.

04

The mechanism: announcements arrive before the signed records

Seventeen rpki_invalid_asn incidents fired in the window. Ten name AS402215 as the invalid party, and they fall into three tight bursts with the same shape. Several blocks go out within seconds of each other. Every one fails RPKI validation because the previous holder’s ROA is still published. The condition then clears within minutes to an hour, when a replacement ROA naming AS402215 appears.

This is precisely the hazard validate_announcement exists to flag. All eight blocks we tested returned verdict warn with the same message: space changed registered holder within 90 days, the previous holder’s ROAs may still be published, confirm before announcing. The announcements went out regardless.

The registry side is directly visible. At least 52 irr_change and 22 roa_change events name AS402215 in the window, adding route objects and signed records for blocks arriving and removing them for blocks leaving. The removals tell as much as the additions. 92.113.46.0/24 and 16.5.1.0/24 both show a ROA naming AS402215 being withdrawn on 8 August as the block moved on to AS142240 and AS139642 respectively. Address space passes through this ASN in both directions.

A health_check run today reports 100% RPKI coverage, 100% IRR coverage and zero MOAS across 87 sampled prefixes. That is a snapshot taken after every burst had already resolved, so it does not contradict the table above. It does show that a hygiene check limited to present state will never surface this pattern.

05

The handovers are contested in both directions

Eighteen moas_conflict incidents fired, 16 with AS402215 as the invalid party. Each marks a period when AS402215 and the previous origin announced the same /24 at once. Between 11:42 and 12:23 UTC on 10 August, eleven blocks were in conflict simultaneously, each seen by 207 to 209 peers across all 24 collectors. That was globally visible rather than a local wobble.

The reverse direction matters too, and reading the direction field rather than inferring roles by hand is what keeps it straight. Seven rpki_invalid_asn incidents place AS402215 in the baseline role, meaning other networks announced space whose current ROA names AS402215. Four of those, all attributed to AS146869, were still active at query time, having run since 10 or 11 August.

One recurring group of ASNs sits on the other side of these transitions throughout the window: AS153168 appears 15 times as a displaced baseline, AS834 14 times, AS146869 12, AS152698 10, AS146857 8, AS25198 7 and AS402487 6. Blocks visibly cycle around this group, and 79.172.192.0/24 passed through four of them in nine days.

The rotation is observable. What connects these networks is not. Routing and registry data cannot establish a relationship between them, and this report asserts none. Shared registration or billing records would settle it.

06

Impact: 93 minutes of nothing on 12 August

Detection counts are not impact. Reachability is. Between 09:56:30 and 11:29:24 UTC on 12 August, 87.83.115.0/24 was unreachable from all 324 peers observing it, reaching 100% at 10:08:54. Over the same window 151.247.1.0/24 lost 310 of its 318 peers, or 97.5%. The two curves track each other second by second going down and coming back.

The reachability response flagged the reason itself: a shared outage window appearing across prefixes points at a common upstream rather than a per-prefix issue. The path data bears that out. Both blocks reach the routing table almost entirely through AS17497 (LGHL-AS-AP, Liasail Global Hongkong Limited), which carries 100% of 151.247.1.0/24’s observed paths and 93% of 87.83.115.0/24’s. When AS17497 stopped carrying them, they left the global table.

Prefixes homed elsewhere were unaffected at that hour. We checked four others across the same day. 2.26.184.0/24 and 81.168.64.0/24 recorded no outage window at all, and 185.209.74.0/24 lost a quarter of its 56 peers for 19 minutes at 08:00, well before the event. Separately, 45.62.168.0/24 lost 247 of 254 peers for 15 minutes on 13 August from 11:44. That prefix transits AS63199, so it is a different upstream and a different outage.

07

Structural fragility, and the limits of this analysis

The outages are not bad luck. A health_check across 87 prefixes found 7 of 8 sampled blocks reaching the routing table through a single upstream, a medium-severity finding and a direct explanation for why one provider’s bad hour removes a set of prefixes outright. The response carried a sampled_checks warning, bounding transit, visibility and unrouted checks to 8 prefixes each, so the true single-homed count across all 94 is unknown.

Filtering is visible as well. 2.26.184.0/24 is seen by 184 peers against a sibling median of 324, or 57%, and the response raised both a single_upstream and an explicit filtered warning. Somebody upstream is dropping this prefix.

AS402215 itself is six months old, registered on 20 February 2026 to Devology Tech LLC, with an abuse contact at devology.tech and no PeeringDB record: no listed facilities and no internet exchange presence. A locate call could not place it, returning an empty facility intersection across its upstreams, and noted that external GeoIP cross-checks are not wired into this API. We have no reliable geography for this network.

Where the evidence runs out

Intent is not observable. Everything above is consistent with an address broker or leasing business operating carelessly, and equally consistent with something less benign wearing correct paperwork. Routing and registry data cannot separate those, and this report does not try.

The transfers were verified on a sample. We ran validate_announcement on 8 of 94 prefixes. Seven returned netutils-mnt with a recent holder change; the eighth, 45.62.168.0/24, had a settled and unrelated holder. Generalising from eight to ninety-four is inference, and the two highlighted rows in section 04 are the kind of case that could break it. Running validate_announcement across the full inventory would settle it.

Short and regional events may be invisible. Route collectors observe a sample of the global routing system. The inventory call also raised a window_start_at_data_floor warning, so a first-seen date landing on the window edge is censored rather than meaningful.

Registry names are labels. DEVOLOGY, netutils-mnt and the upstream names in this report record how resources are registered, not who operates them day to day.

08

Recommendations

Publish the ROA before the announcement, not afterHigh

Ten RPKI-invalid episodes, every one avoidable. In each burst the announcement went out while the previous holder’s ROA was still published, and validating networks dropped the route until a replacement appeared minutes to an hour later. validate_announcement returned warn with this exact caution on all eight blocks tested. Sequencing the ROA first removes the entire category at no cost.

Confirm the previous holder has withdrawn before announcingHigh

Sixteen dual-origin conflicts, eleven of them simultaneous on 10 August, all globally visible. It cuts both ways: AS146857 and AS147276 were still announcing into AS402215’s current ROAs on 13 August, days after the records moved. Make a confirmed withdrawal on the outgoing side a precondition of the incoming announcement.

Add a second upstream to the prefixes that matterMedium

Seven of eight sampled prefixes are single-homed. On 12 August that turned one provider’s problem into 93 minutes of complete unreachability for 87.83.115.0/24 and near-complete for 151.247.1.0/24. On 13 August the same structure cost 45.62.168.0/24 15 minutes through a different provider. A second transit relationship removes the single point of failure.

Find out why 2.26.184.0/24 is being filteredMedium

It is visible to 184 peers where its siblings reach 324, and the platform raised an explicit filtered warning. Check for a stale IRR object, a max-length mismatch on the covering ROA, or an upstream prefix-list left unchanged since the block moved.

For operators peering with or filtering AS402215, treat its prefix list as unstableMedium

Three quarters of this ASN’s announced space is intermittent or transient, and 52 distinct prefixes produced first-time origin claims in eleven days. A filter built from a snapshot of the inventory will be wrong within days. Validate against RPKI at announcement time rather than caching a prefix list.

Re-run this workup over a 60-day windowLow

Eleven days characterises the pattern but cannot date its start, because the inventory call hit the data floor at the window edge. A wider window would show whether the 10 and 12 August waves are the operating norm or an escalation, and would let the irr_invalid_asn counts, not re-queried here, be pinned down exactly.

Sources and scope

Routing data is drawn from public BGP route collectors; registry, RPKI and IRR records from public RDAP, whois and RPKI repositories. The review covers 2026-08-03 to 2026-08-13. Route collectors observe a sample of the global routing system, so a short or regionally contained event may be partly or wholly unobserved. Peer counts describe observation points, not users affected.

Tools used: identify, inventory, detections, platform_baseline, timeline, compare_windows, origin_history, paths, visibility, reachability, validate_announcement, health_check and locate. Counts of prefixes, days, announcements, origins and paths come from pre-aggregated daily rollups. The reachability figures in section 06 are the only numbers reconstructed from individual BGP messages, and no comparison in this report mixes the two. API warnings bearing on a claim are stated where the claim is made: window_start_at_data_floor, sampled_baseline, sampled_checks, multi_prefix, single_upstream, filtered and geoip_unavailable.
